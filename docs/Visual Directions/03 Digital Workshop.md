# Digital Workshop

## Thesis

The portfolio is Nour’s working environment: a deliberately arranged space containing products, experiments, diagrams, games, notes, and tools. Visitors discover how broad exploration feeds one engineering practice.

## Visual Language

- Layered desk or spatial-workbench metaphor without copying an operating-system desktop.
- Artifacts look tactile: diagrams, clipped screenshots, terminal scraps, test reports, and notebook fragments.
- Structured zones prevent the composition from becoming a collage.
- Human annotations reveal decisions and reflection.

## Signature Moments

- Audience choice changes which objects move to the foreground.
- A project opens by expanding its artifact stack into a coherent case study.
- The knowledge section appears as a working library, not a blog grid.
- “How I Work” tutorials feel native because process artifacts already exist in the world.

## Motion

- Objects rearrange based on audience and topic.
- Depth, parallax, and focus guide attention instead of constant movement.
- Dragging may be offered as an enhancement, never required.
- Mobile converts the workshop into ordered shelves and stacks.

## Typography and Color

- Contemporary editorial typography with occasional handwritten-style annotations only if legible in Arabic and English.
- Material colors, paper tones, dark metal, and restrained saturated accents.
- Avoid fake sticky-note clutter and scrapbook clichés.

## Audience Adaptation

- Recruiter desk: CV, .NET projects, roles, and skills are nearest.
- Client desk: briefs, flows, outcomes, and contact are nearest.
- Developer desk: diagrams, code, tests, tutorials, and notes are nearest.

## Risks

- Easy to overdecorate and weaken scanning.
- Spatial interaction can be difficult for keyboard, RTL, and mobile users.
- Requires strong rules for where new content belongs.

## Fit

Best expression of curiosity, fast learning, and the relationship between projects and knowledge sharing.
